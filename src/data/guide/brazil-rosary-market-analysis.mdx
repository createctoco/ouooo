<GuideSection eyebrow="Price structure" title="How the market is layered by price">
  <p>
    Brazilian rosary demand spans a very wide price range, from low-cost rosaries bought by the hundred for parish
    distribution to gift pieces sold individually in a box. The segments are not interchangeable: a buyer sourcing for
    bulk distribution is not comparing the same product as a gift shop, and a quote that is competitive in one segment
    can be uncompetitive in the other.
  </p>
  <p class="mt-5">
    The practical implication is to define the target unit price before choosing the product. Bead material, bead size,
    the crucifix and centerpiece, the cord or chain, and the packaging all move the cost, and the packaging is often the
    easiest place to protect a price point. A simple bag can keep a retail-ready rosary inside a lower band; a printed
    box can push the same rosary into a gift band.
  </p>
</GuideSection>

<GuideSection eyebrow="Competition" title="Where a supplier can differentiate" background="cream">
  <p>
    The Brazilian market is served by a mix of local assembly, domestic wholesalers and imports. At the low end, price
    competition is severe and buyers switch readily. At the middle and upper end, the differentiators are more durable:
    devotional accuracy, consistent quality between orders, packaging that survives retail and shipping, and the ability
    to deliver before a fixed feast date.
  </p>
  <p class="mt-5">
    Language is part of the differentiation. A listing written in Brazilian Portuguese, with the right devotional
    vocabulary and the correct title, reads as a local product rather than an imported one. That is a low-cost advantage
    that many suppliers ignore.
  </p>
</GuideSection>

<GuideSection eyebrow="Language" title="The vocabulary a Brazilian buyer uses">
  <p>
    The everyday Brazilian word for the rosary is <strong>terço</strong>. <em>Rosário</em> is understood, but a listing
    that only uses it will not match the way most shoppers search. Other terms worth using correctly are
    <strong>escapulário</strong> (scapular), <strong>medalha</strong> (medal), <strong>chaveiro</strong> (keychain) and
    <strong>artigos religiosos</strong> (religious goods).
  </p>
  <p class="mt-5">
    Brazilian Portuguese also differs from European Portuguese in vocabulary, spelling and tone. A translation prepared
    in Portugal should be reviewed for Brazil before it is used on packaging or a listing, even when the underlying
    product copy is correct.
  </p>
</GuideSection>

<GuideSection eyebrow="Sourcing implications" title="What this means for a supplier">
  <ul class="grid gap-3">
    <li class="rounded-2xl border border-black/10 bg-white p-4 leading-7 text-muted">
      <strong>Build the range around the calendar.</strong> Plan Aparecida, Fatima, Carmo and the sacramental seasons as
      separate buying windows rather than one continuous season.
    </li>
    <li class="rounded-2xl border border-black/10 bg-white p-4 leading-7 text-muted">
      <strong>Offer at least two price bands.</strong> A bulk rosary and a gift rosary serve different buyers and should
      not be the same product with a different label.
    </li>
    <li class="rounded-2xl border border-black/10 bg-white p-4 leading-7 text-muted">
      <strong>Treat packaging as part of the product.</strong> It sets the retail price band and protects the unit in a
      long distribution chain.
    </li>
    <li class="rounded-2xl border border-black/10 bg-white p-4 leading-7 text-muted">
      <strong>Get the devotion right.</strong> Aparecida, Fatima and Carmo are not interchangeable images, and buyers
      notice.
    </li>
    <li class="rounded-2xl border border-black/10 bg-white p-4 leading-7 text-muted">
      <strong>Quote landed, not just FOB.</strong> Import taxes and freight determine whether a price is really
      competitive in Brazil.
    </li>
  </ul>
</GuideSection>
