  <GuideCallout eyebrow="Why precision matters" title="A cross, a crucifix and a medal are not interchangeable words.">
    In religious goods, the words carry meaning. A buyer looking for a crucifix for a rosary is not looking for a plain
    cross. A parish ordering scapulars is not ordering medals. Getting the term right protects the customer, avoids
    returns, and signals that the supplier understands the products rather than treating them as generic jewellery.
  </GuideCallout>

<GuideSection
  eyebrow="The core distinction"
  title="Cross vs crucifix"
  intro="The difference is simple and visual: does the piece include the figure of Christ?"
>
  <p>
    A cross is a shape. It can be plain, engraved, jewelled or decorative, and it does not by itself tell you whether a
    corpus is present. A crucifix is a cross that bears the corpus, the figure of Christ. Catholics commonly use a
    crucifix for personal devotion and on a rosary, and many Catholic buyers will notice immediately if a listing calls
    a crucifix a cross or the other way around.
  </p>
  <p class="mt-5">
    In a catalogue, the safest approach is to use the most specific term the product supports. If the pendant has a
    corpus, list it as a crucifix. If it does not, list it as a cross. If the product is a shape used across several
    traditions, say so rather than assigning it to one.
  </p>
</GuideSection>

<GuideSection eyebrow="Common mistakes" title="Four errors that show up in religious goods listings" background="cream">
  <p>
    <strong>1. Calling every pendant a cross.</strong> A crucifix without a corpus is a cross, but a cross with a corpus
    is a crucifix. The distinction is visible in the product photo, so it should be visible in the title.
  </p>
  <p class="mt-4">
    <strong>2. Describing a medal as a scapular.</strong> A scapular is a cloth devotional garment. A metal pendant
    shaped like one is a pendant. Mixing the terms misleads buyers who are looking for the traditional object.
  </p>
  <p class="mt-4">
    <strong>3. Listing a component as a finished piece.</strong> A rosary centerpiece is a component used in assembly.
    It is not the same product as a standalone medal, even if the shape is similar.
  </p>
  <p class="mt-4">
    <strong>4. Implying a blessing or approval.</strong> Unless it is documented, an item should not be described as
    blessed, consecrated, relic-bearing or officially approved. Accurate language protects both the buyer and the
    supplier.
  </p>
</GuideSection>

<GuideSection eyebrow="Next step" title="When in doubt, ask for the photo and the specification">
  <p>
    Most terminology problems are resolved by looking at the product image and confirming the construction in writing.
    If a listing is ambiguous, ask the supplier to confirm what the item is, what it is made of, and whether it is a
    finished piece or a component. A short written confirmation is more reliable than an inferred product name.
